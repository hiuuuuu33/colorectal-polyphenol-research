Network-based discovery of bioactive polyphenols based on transcriptomic 
changes across the colorectal adenoma–carcinoma sequence 
 
Abstract 
Colorectal adenomas are major precancerous lesion of colorectal cancer (CRC) 
and provide a clinically actionable window for preventing invasive CRC. However, the 
molecular changes associated with adenoma progression remain incompletely defined. 
Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms underlying adenoma carcinogenesis to identify 
druggable targets is an important strategy for CRC control. We analyzed 33 colorectal 
tissue samples, comprising adenomas from 11 individuals and paired stage-I CRC and 
adjacent normal tissues from an additional 11 patients. Using a network-based 
integrative approach, we identified stage-associated genes underlying the mechanism 
of adenoma carcinogenesis. We identified 725 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) 
in adenoma versus normal tissue, 312 in CRC versus adenoma, and 1,544 in CRC 
versus normal tissue. Twenty-nine genes changed concordantly across the two 
sequential comparisons; all 29 mapped to the interactome used for proximity analysis. 
Adenoma formation was associated with inflammatory recruitment and loss of 
differentiated epithelial and metabolic functions, whereas CRC showed additional 
extracellular matrix, focal adhesion, and cell-cycle activity. Of the 66 compound entries 
screened, 11 unique compounds are identified. Kaempferol showed the strongest 
network proximity, followed by 4-methylcatechol, quercetin, punicalagin, and 
umbelliferone. Collectively, this study provides a novel network medicine-based 
approach applicable to the prevention and treatment of CRC. 
 
Keywords: colorectal adenoma; colorectal cancer; transcriptomics; network medicine; 
polyphenols; molecular docking

1. Introduction 
Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a major global health burden. GLOBOCAN 
2022 estimated more than 1.9 million new CRC cases and approximately 904,000 
deaths worldwide, making CRC the third most frequently diagnosed cancer and the 
second leading cause of cancer death (Bray et al., 2024). In China, an estimated 517,100 
new CRC cases and 240,000 deaths occurred in 2022 (Han et al., 2024). 
Most sporadic CRCs arise through multistage transformation of non-neoplastic 
colorectal epithelium into an adenoma and invasive carcinoma. The conventional 
adenoma–carcinoma pathway accounts for approximately 90% of the sporadic CRC 
precursors and is commonly initiated by APC/WNT dysregulation and followed by 
genetic and epigenetic alterations affecting RAS–MAPK, PI3K, TGF-β and TP53-
associated processes (Keum and Giovannucci, 2019). Importantly, progression from a 
premalignant lesion to invasive cancer generally requires at least 10 years. Thus, there 
is a unique window of opportunity for secondary prevention of CRC (Keum and 
Giovannucci, 2019; Nguyen et al., 2020). 
Current prevention takes advantage of this window mainly through colonoscopic 
detection and endoscopic removal of adenomas (Kaltenbach et al., 2020), yet with 
limited options for directly interfering with the molecular processes that drive adenoma 
progression. After removing visible adenomas, new metachronous adenomas may still 
develop. A meta-analysis of placebo groups in post-polypectomy trials estimated that 
approximately 60% of patients had adenoma recurrence or detection within five years, 
and about 14% developed advanced adenomas during the same period (Shi et al., 2017). 
These findings suggest that endoscopic removal alone may be insufficient and that 
additional preventive strategies are needed. 
Polyphenols are a diverse group of plant-derived natural compounds that have 
attracted increasing attention as potential agents for colorectal cancer prevention and 
treatment, such as polyphenol-based nanoparticles (Wahnou et al., 2023). Experimental 
studies have shown that polyphenols exhibit multiple bioactive properties that affect 
processes closely related to tumor development, including inflammation, oxidative 
stress, cell proliferation, apoptosis, and cancer-associated signaling pathways, which 
matches the biological complexity of adenoma carcinoma progression (Wahnou et al., 
2023). 
In addition, network pharmacology offers a systematic approach for connecting 
candidate compounds, predicted targets, disease-related genes, protein–protein 
interaction networks, and enriched biological pathways (Zhou et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 
2023). Applying network pharmacology to colorectal adenoma carcinoma progression 
may help identify critical pathway modules and potential polyphenol compounds 
involved in colorectal carcinogenesis, providing a basis for further experimental 
validation. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1 Data source and study design 
Processed RNA-sequencing expression data were obtained from GEO accession 
GSE228012 (SRA PRJNA940523) by Lin et al. (2025). The dataset comprised 
adenomas from 11 individuals and paired stage-I CRC and adjacent normal tissues from 
an additional 11 patients. Thus, the adenoma group was independent of the CRC–
normal patient pairs. GEO is maintained as a public functional-genomics repository 
(Edgar et al., 2002). 
The compound–target resource followed the curation workflow of do Valle et al. 
(2021). Food polyphenols were identified from Phenol-Explorer (Neveu et al., 2010),

mapped to PubChem, filtered using Comparative Toxicogenomics Database disease 
annotations (Davis et al., 2023), and required experimentally supported protein-binding 
information in STITCH 5 (Szklarczyk et al., 2016). The source workflow reported 65 
polyphenols and 598 protein targets; the present proximity output contained 66 rows 
because one compound representation was duplicated before identity normalization. 
2.2 Discovery of molecular mechanisms related to colorectal carcinogenesis 
The processed FPKM expression matrix was downloaded from the GEO 
supplementary files and analyzed using R. Duplicate gene symbols were removed by 
retaining the gene with the highest average expression level. Genes with low expression 
levels were filtered out, and expression values were transformed using log2(FPKM+1) 
before downstream analysis. 
Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package (Ritchie 
et al., 2015). Three comparisons were conducted: CRC versus normal, adenoma versus 
normal, and CRC versus adenoma. Genes with a Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P value 
< 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 1 were considered significantly differentially expressed. 
Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using FactoMineR and visualized 
with factoextra (Lê et al., 2008; Kassambara and Mundt, 2020) based on the log2-
transformed expression matrix. Volcano plots were generated using ggplot2 (Wickham, 
2016) to show upregulated, downregulated and non-significant genes in each 
comparison. 
The results were further screened for genes associated with adenoma–carcinoma 
progression. Genes significantly upregulated in both the normal-to-adenoma and 
adenoma-to-CRC transitions were defined as progressively upregulated, whereas genes 
significantly downregulated in both transitions were defined as progressively 
downregulated. This yielded 29 progression-associated genes (8 up–up and 21 down–
down). Heatmaps were constructed using pheatmap (Kolde, 2019). 
Functional enrichment analysis was performed using clusterProfiler (Yu et al., 
2012). Gene symbols were converted to Entrez identifiers using the org.Hs.eg.db 
annotation package (Carlson, n.d.). Gene Ontology biological-process, cellular-
component and molecular-function analyses used the mapped genes from each 
complete DEG set (Gene Ontology Consortium, 2021), whereas KEGG analysis was 
performed separately for mapped upregulated and downregulated genes (Kanehisa et 
al., 2021). Terms and pathways with Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P < 0.05 were 
considered significant. 
 
2.3 PPI construction 
The 29 progression-associated genes were submitted to STRING, with the 
organism restricted to Homo sapiens (Szklarczyk et al., 2023). The interaction network 
was imported into Cytoscape for visualization and topological analysis (Shannon et al., 
2003). Degree, betweenness centrality and closeness centrality were calculated for each 
node; genes with the highest degree values were treated as candidate hubs. The 
functional-association network was set at confidential scores of 0.150. 
The score-filtered STRING node and edge tables were imported into Cytoscape 
for visualization and topological analysis (Shannon et al., 2003). Degree was defined 
as the number of edges incident on a node; betweenness centrality as the proportion of 
shortest paths passing through a node; and closeness centrality as the reciprocal of mean 
shortest-path distance to reachable nodes. Centrality was calculated on the same score-
filtered graph.

2.4 Network proximity between polyphenol targets and colorectal cancer protein 
To connect the transcriptomic alterations identified across the adenoma–carcinoma 
sequence with candidate polyphenols, we applied the network-proximity framework 
described by Guney et al. (2016) and subsequently adapted for polyphenols by do Valle 
et al. (2021). The analysis was performed on the human interactome assembled by do 
Valle et al. (2021), which integrates experimentally supported physical protein–protein 
interactions from 16 databases and contains a largest connected component of 17,651 
proteins connected by 351,393 interactions. In this network, proteins are represented as 
nodes and experimentally supported protein–protein interactions as edges. 
The 29 progression-associated genes identified from the transcriptomic analysis 
were mapped to their corresponding proteins in this interactome. The experimentally 
supported targets of each polyphenol were mapped to the same network. Network 
proximity was then used to quantify how closely the polyphenol targets were positioned 
relative to the progression-associated proteins. 
For each polyphenol target, the shortest path to the nearest progression-associated 
protein was calculated. The network proximity was defined as:

2.5 Molecular docking evaluation 
The crystal structures of colorectal cancer related proteins were obtained from the 
RCSB Protein Data Bank (Niederhut et al., 2001; Boyles et al., 2020). Protein structures 
were prepared using AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 (Morris et al., 2009) by removing water 
molecules and crystallographic ligands, adding polar hydrogen atoms, assigning 
Gasteiger charges, and exporting the receptors in PDBQT format. In the current 
analysis, NAD and zinc ions were not retained in the prepared 1HT0 structure, whereas 
the complete chain A–F assembly of 6WZM was used as the receptor. 
The three-dimensional structures of the selected phenolic compounds were 
downloaded from PubChem in SDF format and converted using Open Babel (O’Boyle 
et al., 2011). The ligands were subsequently prepared in AutoDock Tools by assigning 
Gasteiger charges and atom types and defining eligible rotatable bonds as flexible

before conversion to PDBQT format. Molecular docking was performed using 
AutoDock Vina 1.1.2 (Trott and Olson, 2010). The docking search space was defined 
using a grid centre of original ligand. The exhaustiveness was set to 8, and the binding 
modes were generated for each ligand. For each receptor–ligand pair, the pose with the 
lowest predicted Vina score was selected for interaction analysis. Protein–ligand 
interactions were visualized using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer version. 
Docking scores were expressed in kcal/mol and used only for relative candidate 
prioritization within each receptor system. 
3. Results 
3.1 Transcriptomic changes during adenoma carcinoma progression 
To capture the molecular features of colorectal adenoma-carcinoma progression, 
we analyzed transcriptomic data spanning the adenoma-carcinoma sequence. The 
dataset contained expression profiles for 18,116 genes across 33 colorectal tissue 
samples, including 11 normal samples, 11 adenoma samples, and 11 CRC samples. 
Principal component analysis was performed to assess the overall transcriptomic 
differences among normal colorectal tissue, adenoma, and CRC samples. Normal 
samples formed a relatively compact cluster and were clearly separated from most 
adenoma and CRC samples. Adenoma samples occupied an intermediate region, 
whereas CRC samples displayed a broader distribution and partially overlapped with 
the adenoma group. These results indicate that substantial transcriptomic changes had 
already occurred at the adenoma stage, followed by additional heterogeneous 
alterations compared to mature CRC samples (Fig. 1A). 
Differential expression analysis revealed obvious transcriptional changes at 
different stages of colorectal tumorigenesis. Substantial transcriptional alterations were 
already evident in adenomas relative to normal tissue, and we identified in total 725 
differentially expressed genes. Among the most prominently downregulated genes were 
CA1, CHGA, GUCA2A, SLC26A3, and ZG16, whereas MMP7, TCN1, DUOXA2, 
DEFA6, and REG4 showed increased expression (Fig. 1B). The comparison between 
CRC and adenoma identified 312 differentially expressed genes associated with 
malignant progression. THBS2, CXCL8, COL1A1, PCSK9, and EREG were among 
the prominently upregulated genes in CRC, while CLCA1, FCGBP, DEFA6, SPINK4, 
and DEFA5 were downregulated. The genes highlighted in this comparison differed 
partly from those identified during adenoma formation, suggesting that the two 
transitions involve largely different transcriptional changes (Fig. 1C). The direct 
comparison between CRC and normal tissue revealed the accumulated expression 
changes across the complete disease sequence. CDH3, CEMIP, CXCL8, MMP7, and 
DPEP1 were markedly upregulated in CRC, whereas CA1, AQP8, CHGA, ZG16, and 
SLC26A3 were among the most strongly downregulated genes. Several of these genes 
were also altered in the sequential comparisons, indicating that part of the 
transcriptional disturbance initiated at the adenoma stage was maintained or further 
enhanced in CRC (Fig. 1D). 
However, we observed that not all differentially expressed genes followed a 
continuous pattern across the three stages. For example, DEFA6 was upregulated in 
adenoma relative to normal tissue but downregulated in CRC relative to adenoma. This 
stage specific change suggests that some genes may be associated primarily with 
adenoma formation solely, but not during the whole development. To identify genes 
more closely associated with continuous disease progression, genes showing 
concordant expression changes across normal tissue, adenoma, and CRC were further 
screened. A total of 29 genes exhibited progressive expression patterns, including eight

progressively upregulated genes and 21 progressively downregulated genes. The 
progressively upregulated genes were CXCL2, SLC22A3, HIST3H2A, SLCO4A1, 
MYC, COL12A1, TNFRSF12A, and CXCL8 (Fig. 2A), while down regulated genes 
included JCHAIN, ST6GALNAC6, PADI2, ADH1C, LYPD8, HSD17B2, MFSD4A, 
GPT, TNFRSF17, SCIN, BEST2, and ADTRP (Fig. 2B).  
Together, these results revealed both stage-specific changes and a smaller set of 
29 genes that change consistently across all three stages. Here, we use these 29 genes 
as the progression-associated set for the network analyses subsequently. To understand 
the functional changes behind these expression patterns, we next asked which 
biological processes and pathways are affected at each stage.

3.2 Functional changes associated with adenoma formation and carcinoma 
The differential expression patterns identified across the normal–adenoma–CRC 
sequence suggested distinct biological changes at different stages of colorectal 
tumorigenesis.  To investigate whether these transcriptional alterations were associated 
with biological mechanisms implicated in colorectal cancer development, we 
performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the differentially expressed genes 
from each sequential comparison.  
In the adenoma-versus-normal comparison, 239 of 4,540 tested GO biological 
processes, 21 of 381 cellular components and 68 of 766 molecular functions met the 
adjusted-P threshold (Table 1). Representative biological processes included humoral 
immune response, leukocyte chemotaxis, extracellular-matrix organization and 
detoxification, whereas collagen-containing extracellular matrix and apical plasma 
membrane were prominent cellular components; extracellular-matrix structural 
constituent and cytokine activity were representative molecular functions (Fig. 3A-C; 
Table 1). 
KEGG analysis of the same contrast identified 10 significant pathways among 211 
tested for upregulated genes and seven among 283 tested for downregulated genes (Fig. 
3D-E; Table 1). Upregulated pathways included cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, 
IL-17 signalling and NOD-like receptor signalling, while downregulated pathways 
included mineral absorption, bile secretion, fat digestion and absorption, and pentose 
and glucuronate interconversions (Table 1). Together, these results indicate that 
inflammatory recruitment and extracellular-matrix changes coexist with loss of 
differentiated epithelial and metabolic functions at the adenoma stage.

3.3 PPI network of progression-associated genes 
To determine whether the 29 progression associated genes identified from the 
sequential transcriptomic analysis represented an interconnected biological module, we 
examined the functional relationships among their encoded proteins using STRING. 
We further characterized the topology of this network to identify proteins occupying 
central positions within the progression associated module. This analysis was intended 
to provide a network-level view of the molecular alterations that persist across the 
normal–adenoma–CRC sequence and to complement the subsequent human protein 
interactome-based compound proximity analysis. 
All 29 progression-associated proteins were represented in the STRING network, 
which contained 61 observed interactions compared with 28 expected interactions for 
a random protein set of the same size (Fig. 5A). The average node degree in our network 
was 4.21 and the local clustering coefficient was 0.577. The network showed significant 
PPI enrichment (P = 2.99 × 10⁻⁸), supporting functional connectivity among the 
progression-associated proteins. 
We next examined network topology to identify proteins with potentially central 
positions within these 29 progression-associated protein modules. Degree centrality 
reflects the number of direct connections of a protein, whereas betweenness centrality 
reflects the extent to which a protein lies on shortest paths connecting other proteins. 
As shown in Fig. 5B, topological analysis identified MYC and CXCL8 as the most

highly connected nodes, each with a degree of 13. MYC showed the highest 
betweenness centrality (0.298), followed by CXCL8 (0.261), while both genes had a 
closeness centrality of 0.657. 
The tendency of degree and betweenness centrality of key targets is highly 
correlated. However, some of the nodes possess high betweenness centrality values 
despite relatively low degree values. For example, CA12, with a degree of only 3, 
ranked ninth in betweenness centrality, underscoring its role as a bridge between 
otherwise separated network components. The top ten degree-ranked targets in the 
network are MYC, CXCL8, GPT, UGT1A10, PPARGC1A, TNFRSF17, ADH1C, 
CXCL2, HSD17B2, and FABP2, indicating their essential roles for the network.

3.4 Proximity between polyphenol targets and colorectal proteins 
That the 29 progression-associated proteins form a connected network invites a 
practical question: which dietary polyphenols act on proteins close to this network?  To 
address this, we used network proximity to rank polyphenols by how close their protein 
targets lie to the progression-associated proteins. 
Polyphenols can be viewed as drugs in that they bind to specific proteins, affecting 
their ability to perform their normal functions. Previous work has shown that the 
network proximity between polyphenol targets and disease-associated proteins can 
predict known therapeutic effects of polyphenols, with closer proximity generally 
associated with a greater likelihood of affecting the disease phenotype (do Valle et al., 
2021). Based on this framework, we examined whether the targets of individual 
polyphenols were unusually close to the 29 progression-associated proteins identified 
in our analysis, thereby prioritizing compounds potentially relevant to adenoma–
carcinoma progression. 
We therefore calculated the network proximity between polyphenol targets and 
proteins associated with 29 colorectal progression-associated genes to predict diseases 
for which specific polyphenols might have therapeutic effects, using the closest 
measure, dc, representing the average shortest path length between each polyphenol 
target and the nearest disease protein (Methods).

Among the 66 polyphenols compound analyzed, 12 met the predefined network-
proximity threshold (Z < −1.96), indicating that their protein targets were located 
significantly closer to the progression-associated disease module than expected by 
chance. In this framework, greater network proximity suggests a stronger potential 
relationship between a compound and the molecular processes underlying adenoma–
carcinoma progression. Resveratrol and trans-resveratrol shared the same PubChem 
CID (445154), identical target sets, and identical proximity scores, indicating that they 
represented the same compound. After merging these duplicate records, 11 unique 
compounds remained above the significance threshold.

3.5 Molecular docking analysis 
To complement the network-based prioritization with a structure assessment, 
molecular docking was performed for the ten prioritized compounds against selected 
progression-associated proteins. The analysis was used to examine whether these 
compounds could adopt plausible binding poses and to compare their predicted docking 
scores within each receptor system. CXCL8 and ADH1C were selected as 
representative targets for structural evaluation based on their relatively high 
connectivity within the progression-associated PPI network, with degree values of 13 
and 7, respectively. Both also had experimentally resolved protein structures available 
for molecular docking. 
For the complete 6WZM CXCL8-Fab assembly, the ten primary compound records 
produced best-mode scores ranging from -6.1 to -2.6 kcal/mol (5). Phloretin had the 
most favourable score within this receptor system (-6.1 kcal/mol), followed by trans-
resveratrol (-5.9 kcal/mol), kaempferol (-5.7 kcal/mol), and quercetin and esculetin 
(both -5.6 kcal/mol). Within the predefined interface-centred search box, the 
representative phloretin pose was positioned between CXCL8 chain F and Fab heavy

4. Discussion 
This study integrates cross-sectional transcriptomic comparisons across normal 
colorectal tissue, adenoma and CRC with interactome-based compound prioritization. 
The largest expression shift occurred between normal tissue and adenoma, whereas 
fewer genes differed between adenoma and CRC. Functional enrichment suggests that 
inflammatory recruitment and loss of mature epithelial transport and metabolic 
programs are established in adenomas, while carcinoma is associated with additional 
collagen organization, focal adhesion and cell-cycle activity. 
The 29 progression-associated genes identified here are supported by previous 
research. MYC is a WNT-responsive oncogenic mediator whose deletion rescues major 
phenotypes caused by acute Apc loss in the mouse intestine (Sansom et al., 2007). 
CXCL8 has been associated with advanced stage, stromal abundance and metastatic 
features in human CRC tissue (Pennel et al., 2022). COL12A1 has been detected in 
desmoplastic stroma and at invasive fronts (Karagiannis et al., 2012). Conversely, 
progressive loss of CA12, HSD17B2, ADH1C, UGT1A10 and FABP2 is compatible 
with loss of differentiated epithelial functions. 
Among the prioritized compounds, trans-Resveratrol had the strongest direct 


PDF PAGE 22
22 
 
human evidence, with both the parent compound and its metabolites detected in 
colorectal tissue and a modest reduction in tumour Ki-67 levels after eight days (Patel 
et al., 2010). Esculetin exhibited activity in an orthotopic CRC mouse model (Kim et 
al., 2018), and umbelliferone reduced aberrant crypt foci in a chemically induced rat 
model (Muthu et al., 2016). Kaempferol, punicalagin, 2,3-dihydroxybenzoic acid, and 
phloretin were supported primarily by cell-based or early preclinical studies (Yoshida 
et al., 2008; Sun et al., 2023; Sankaranarayanan et al., 2020; Kapoor and Padwad, 2023). 
Quercetin was evaluated only as part of a five-patient curcumin combination study, so 
its independent contribution could not be determined (Cruz-Correa et al., 2006). 
Caution is warranted for 4-Methylcatechol, which is a simple microbial phenolic 
metabolite; although its sulfate metabolite correlated with tumour apoptosis markers 
after a black-raspberry intervention, chronic high-dose dietary exposure caused 
forestomach and glandular-stomach tumours in rats (Asakawa et al., 1994; Pan et al., 
2015). These compounds may remain in the computational output but should not be 
advanced as equivalent preventive candidates. 
Based on molecular docking results, kaempferol was predicted to bind to the active 
site of ADH1C. Kaempferol exerts multifaceted chemopreventive and therapeutic 
effects against CRC. It modulates key epigenetic mechanisms, induces apoptosis, 
suppresses inflammation and oxidative stress, and inhibits tumor cell proliferation, 
thereby interfering with multiple hallmarks of CRC progression (Chen et al., 2026). In 
addition, kaempferol has been identified as an alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) inhibitor 
that acts via mixed-type inhibition against ADH1C and other ADH isoforms (Su et al., 
2025). By inhibiting ADH1C-mediated ethanol metabolism, kaempferol may reduce 
the formation of acetaldehyde, a carcinogenic metabolite, and thus potentially attenuate 
alcohol-associated colorectal carcinogenesis. Collectively, these findings suggest that 
kaempferol may prevent CRC through both direct tumor-suppressive mechanisms and 
indirect modulation of ethanol metabolism, although further experimental and clinical 
studies are warranted to validate these effects. 
The principal limitations are the small sample size and lack of external 
transcriptomic or protein-level experimental validation. Before strong biomarker 
claims, the CRC–normal contrast should be repeated with patient blocking and the 29-
gene module tested in an independent adenoma–CRC cohort. Compound validation 
should compare normal colon organoids, adenoma organoids and CRC models at 
exposure-relevant concentrations, followed by target-engagement, pharmacokinetic 
and toxicological assessment. 
 
 
5. Conclusion 
In this study, we applied an integrative network-medicine framework combining 
paired transcriptomic profiling with interactome-based compound prioritization to 
normal colorectal tissue, adenoma and CRC. Adenoma formation was characterized by 
inflammatory recruitment and loss of differentiated epithelial and metabolic programs, 
whereas progression to carcinoma was associated with additional extracellular matrix 
organization, focal adhesion, and cell-cycle activity. Among the 29 concordantly 
changed genes across sequential comparisons, network proximity analysis identified 11 
unique candidate compounds, with kaempferol showing the strongest proximity. 
Molecular docking further predicted that kaempferol binds to the active site of ADH1C, 
suggesting a potential dual mechanism involving direct tumor-suppressive effects and 
inhibition of ADH1C-mediated ethanol metabolism. Collectively, this work provides a

novel network-based strategy for CRC prevention and highlights kaempferol as a 
promising candidate that merits further studies.

As this paper draws to a close, I would like to express my sincere gratitude to all 
the teachers, family members, and friends who provided guidance, assistance, and 
support throughout this research. 
 
This study originated from an experience that was deeply personal to me. My 
grandmother was diagnosed with colon cancer in 2019 and underwent prolonged 
treatment in the years that followed. She ultimately passed away from the disease in 
2025. This experience had a profound impact on me and led me to develop an early 
interest in colorectal cancer, which later became one of the main reasons I chose this 
research topic. From her attending physician, I learned that most sporadic colorectal 
cancers do not arise suddenly but instead develop through a multistep process from 
normal mucosa to invasive carcinoma. This led me to focus on identifying gene-
expression changes underlying this progression. 
 
As I explored the literature further, I became familiar with approaches in network 
pharmacology and network medicine. At the same time, having grown up in 
Guangdong, I had already developed an interest in traditional Chinese medicine and 
bioactive compounds derived from natural products. I therefore began with a relatively 
broad investigation of naturally derived compounds. As my literature review 
progressed, I gradually narrowed the scope of the study to polyphenolic compounds. 
 
The entire research project lasted approximately three months and was carried out 
independently by me. During the early stage, I focused on defining the research 
direction, reviewing literature related to the adenoma–carcinoma progression of 
colorectal cancer, and identifying suitable publicly available datasets. I then used 
publicly available transcriptomic datasets containing samples from normal colorectal 
tissue, adenomas, and early-stage colorectal cancer, obtained from the Gene 
Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, to perform differential gene-expression 
analysis. This analysis ultimately identified 29 progression-associated genes, which 
were used as the primary disease-related protein set for the subsequent network analysis. 
 
In the next stage of the study, these progression-associated proteins and the known 
protein targets of polyphenols were mapped onto the human protein–protein interaction 
network. Network distance was then used to evaluate the proximity between different 
candidate compounds and proteins associated with colorectal cancer progression. 
During data preparation and analysis, I encountered several issues, including duplicate 
compound names and substantial variation in the number of known targets associated 
with different compounds. To address these problems, I carefully verified compound 
identities and repeatedly checked the corresponding target-protein information.

progression through network pharmacology, a teacher at my school recommended that 
I contact Professor Chen, who had previously been their colleague. Given his expertise 
in this field, I proactively reached out to him to discuss my research ideas and seek 
academic guidance.

Both teachers served primarily in advisory roles by providing methodological and

The research process also involved many challenges. During the transcriptomic 
analysis stage, I had to learn data-processing and statistical methods in R with which I 
had little experience. When I moved on to network analysis, I needed to understand 
new concepts such as the human interactome, shortest-path network distance, 
randomization, and Z-scores. During the later stages of writing, I repeatedly assessed 
whether the predictions generated by the network analysis were consistent with existing 
experimental and clinical evidence. I addressed these challenges mainly by reading 
original research papers, discussing methodological questions with my supervisors, and 
revising and checking my analyses. 
 
I would also like to give special thanks to my family and friends for their 
companionship and support throughout these four months of research. Because this 
project was directly connected to my own family experience, reviewing clinical 
information about the progression of colorectal cancer sometimes had a strong 
emotional impact on me, and at one point I found it difficult to continue writing. The 
understanding and encouragement of my family and friends helped me return to the 
research and persevere until the project was completed. For me, this study was not only 
an academic exploration, but also a process of learning how to confront a personal 
experience and search for answers through research.
